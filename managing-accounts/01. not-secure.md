# Your Account Is Only As Safe As Its Weakest Way In

Every account has a front door (how you log in) and a back door (how you get back in when you forget). Attackers use whichever is easier.

**A strong lock on the front door does nothing if "forgot my password" sends a text message.**

## Text message codes are not safe

A texted code is a temporary password sent in plain text to a phone number. As far as the website is concerned, whoever has the code is you.

Four ways someone else gets it:

* **You read it to them.** A caller claiming to be your bank asks for "the code we just sent." This is the most common attack. No real company will ever call you and ask for a code.
* **They take your number.** A criminal talks your carrier into moving your number to their phone (a "SIM swap"). It can cost them little or nothing.
* **They intercept it inside the phone network.** The system carriers use to route texts between networks (called SS7) was built decades ago with no real security. Criminals rent access to it for about $50. Your text is copied in transit. Your phone is never touched, your carrier account is never touched, no PIN or lock stops it, and you will never know it happened.
* **They get into your phone.** A short PIN may unlock your texts, email, and saved passwords all at once.

Recovery email has the same problem. It is only as safe as the phone or computer where that mailbox is already open.

### If there is real money behind the account

**Text codes are not protection. Assume anyone willing to spend $50 can read them.**

A text code stops a bored stranger with a leaked password. It does not stop someone who picked you on purpose. If an account holds your savings, and a text can log in, approve a transfer, or reset the password, then that account is protected by a text message and nothing else.

For those accounts the question is not "how do I make texting safer." It is "does this institution let me turn texting off?" If the answer is no, that is a reason to move the money.

For low-value accounts, a text code is still better than a password alone. Do not switch it off unless you are replacing it with something stronger.

## Where the secret lives (weakest to strongest)

Assumes each password is long and used on only one site.

| Rank | Where | Why |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | **A code texted to your phone** | Travels through the phone network in plain text. Read by anyone who rents network access (about $50), swaps your SIM, or talks you into reading it aloud. NIST restricted it in 2017. CISA said stop using it in 2024. |
| 1 | **`ALL MY PASSWORDS.txt` on the computer** | Any malware on that computer gets the whole collection at once. |
| 2 | **Saved in the browser (Chrome, Edge)** | Encrypted, but usually unlocked for whoever or whatever is logged into the computer. |
| 3 | **Paper in a drawer** | No Internet attacker can reach it. Weak only against people inside your home. |
| 4 | **Paper in a lockbox** | Same, with physical protection. |
| 5 | **Password manager with a strong master password** | Encrypted, creates unique random passwords, and refuses to fill on a look-alike site, which is a useful warning sign. |


However you store it, a password can still be typed into a fake website. That is the limit of passwords.

## How you prove it's you (weakest to strongest)

| Rank | Method | Why |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Password only** | One stolen or tricked-out secret and they are in. |
| 2 | **Password + text code** | Stops casual attackers only. The code can be read to a scammer, typed into a fake site, stolen by SIM swap, or intercepted in the network. **Not acceptable for accounts holding real money.** |
| 3 | **[Password + authenticator app](<02. authenticator.md>)** | Codes never travel through your phone number. You can still be tricked into typing one into a fake site, or approving a prompt you did not start. |
| 4 | **[Synced passkey](<03. passkeys.md>) (Apple, Google, password manager)** | Nothing to type, nothing to read aloud, works only on the real site. Its safety depends on your Apple or Google account and how that account is recovered. **Best choice for most people.** |
| 5 | **[Hardware security key](<03. passkeys.md>) (e.g. YubiKey)** | The secret never leaves the key and cannot be copied. Buy two. Lose your only key and you are back to the recovery path. |

## The part most people miss

**Passkey + text message recovery = text message security.**

If an account lets someone skip the passkey using a text or an already-open email account, that weaker path is the real security of the account.

This is why a password on paper in a drawer can be safer against an Internet attacker than an account that resets by text.

## What to do this week

1. **Never read a code to anyone who contacted you.** Hang up and call the number on your card.
2. **Lock your phone number.** Call your carrier and ask for a port-out PIN or number lock (carriers call it Number Lock, SIM Protection, or Account Lock). This blocks SIM swaps. It does nothing against network interception.
3. **Use a longer phone PIN.** Six digits or more, not a birthday.
4. **Secure your email first.** It is the back door to everything else. Add a passkey or authenticator app, then check its recovery options.
5. **Check the back doors.** On your bank, brokerage, email, and Apple or Google account, look at the login and recovery settings. Remove your phone number as a login or recovery method wherever allowed.
6. **Ask your bank and brokerage directly:** "Can I use an authenticator app, passkey, or security key, and turn text codes off completely?" If they say no, no setting on your side fixes that. Decide whether that is where your savings should live.
