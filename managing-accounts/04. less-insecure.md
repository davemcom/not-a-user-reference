# Security Plan for Real Money

Last updated 2026-09-19

This plan is for anyone whose accounts could lose a life's work in one afternoon. It is [more than the basic checklist](<01. not-secure.md>) and less than what people who run systems for a living need: three rings, three keys, one password manager, and a handoff arranged now, while you can still arrange it.


## Where this fits

The standard is set by the attacker, not by you. A bored stranger with a leaked password gives up at the first obstacle. Someone who has read about you will spend real money and months. If losing an account would be a meaningful loss, plan for the second kind of attacker.

| Tier | Who it is for | Keys | Rings | Text message recovery | Handoff |
| --- | --- | --: | --: | --- | --- |
| [The checklist](<01. not-secure.md>) | Everyone | 0 | 1 | Off where allowed | None |
| **This plan** | Anyone who could lose a life's work in an afternoon | 3 | 3 | Off everywhere, or the money moves | Arranged now, in writing |
| Operator | People who run systems for a living | 17 | 6 | Never existed | Per ring |

The line between the first two tiers is simple. If you would pay a lawyer to get an account back, you are in this tier. Most of us set up these accounts years ago, when the stakes were smaller, the attacks were simpler, and text message codes were thought to be safe. The setup did not keep pace. That is normal, and it is fixable in two afternoons.

## Three rings, three keys

A ring is a group of accounts that fall together. Three is enough for one person, because the email and the money fall together anyway.

| Ring | What is in it | Protected by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Money | Brokerage, bank, retirement, trust accounts, any crypto custody | Hardware key. Text messages off. Transfers need a phone callback. |
| Identity | Primary email, phone carrier account, Apple or Google account, the password manager | Same hardware key. Text messages off. This ring is the back door to Money, so it gets the same lock. |
| Everything else | Shopping, news, streaming, social | Password manager and passkeys. No hardware key needed. |

Three identical hardware keys (YubiKey 5C NFC works on phone and laptop):

1. **Everyday key** on your keyring. Tap it to log in.
2. **Spare key** in the home safe.
3. **Handoff key** in the bank safe deposit box, in a sealed envelope with the printed recovery codes. Whoever takes over gets to this box through the paperwork in the handoff section, not through you.

If a key is more than you will manage, the fallback is a synced passkey on your iPhone with Apple's recovery contact and recovery key set up. That is good. But then Apple's recovery process is your recovery process, and it is worth knowing that.

## Every key on every account

The three keys only work as a set if every account knows all three. A spare that was never registered is not a spare. It is a piece of plastic in a safe.

- The day the keys arrive, sit down with all three and register all three on each account in Money and Identity, one account at a time. Not one now and the others later.
- Finish the whole setup before the handoff key goes into the bank box. Once it is there, every new account means a trip to the bank, so do it once.
- Keep a printed list: each account, and a check mark for each key on it. No passwords on it. It lives with the handoff key.
- A new account in Money or Identity gets all three keys before the first dollar goes in.
- Lose a key: log in with the spare, remove the lost key from every account on the list, order a replacement, register it everywhere. You never sit at one key.
- Test it. After text messages are off, log out and back in with the everyday key, then again with the spare. If the spare fails anywhere, it was never a spare.
- Twice a year, log in once with the spare on the money accounts. Keys do not wear out, but institutions change their systems and settings drift.

## One password manager, one root

Three password managers, a spreadsheet, a notebook, and the browser's saved list are not five layers of safety. They are five copies of the same keys, and the attacker only needs the worst-kept one.

- Pick one manager (1Password or Bitwarden; both offer a family plan and emergency access). Everything goes in it. Nothing lives anywhere else.
- Move the spreadsheet and the notebook into it, then destroy them. Delete the spreadsheet and its backups. Shred the pages. One copy on paper in a safe is fine; it is the fourth and fifth copies scattered around that make you easy.
- Turn off saved passwords in Chrome, Edge and Safari, and delete what they hold. The manager fills passwords now.
- Cancel the other managers after the move, and delete their vaults.
- The one password you still memorize is the manager's. Make it a sentence: five or six unrelated words, at least 20 characters. A word that means something to you is fine as one of the five. It cannot be the whole password.
- Write that sentence once, on paper, sealed in the envelope with the handoff key. Not in a file, not in a text to your daughter.
- The manager itself is in the Identity ring: hardware key on, text message recovery off.

After this there is exactly one root. That is the point. One root, guarded like the money, is safer than five roots guarded like a magazine subscription.

## Money that moves with a login

Cash, securities and crypto can leave with one successful login. For these accounts you are not a customer choosing settings. You are a client telling the institution what it must do. Ask for each of these by name, in writing, and keep the reply.

| Ask for | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Hardware key or authenticator app login, and text message codes turned off completely | If they cannot turn texts off, the account is protected by a text. Move the money to a custodian who can. |
| Verbal password on the phone channel, and no changes by phone without it | The phone line is a second front door. Most resets that succeed happen there. |
| Callback on every outgoing wire or new transfer destination, to the number on file, never to a number in the request | This defeats the attacker who has your email but not your phone. |
| A transfer lockdown or daily limit on electronic withdrawals, with a delay to lift it | A stolen login then moves nothing today. Delay is protection. |
| A trusted contact person on file | The firm can call them if something looks wrong, before money moves. US brokerages are required to ask for one. |
| Statements and alerts to a second person you choose | Fraud is caught by whoever reads the statements. Make that two people. |

A custodian that refuses most of this list is telling you what your account is worth to them. Believe them.

## Money that moves with a signature

Real estate, private holdings and partnership interests do not leave with a login. They leave with a forged instruction that your attorney, accountant, or assistant believes came from you. The larger pile is usually here, and it is attacked through the people around you, not through you.

- **The callback rule, for everyone who can move your money.** Any instruction to send funds, change wiring details, or sign a transfer is confirmed by a phone call to a known number before it is acted on. Every time, including when the email is from you. Say this to each of them in person and put it in your engagement letters.
- **Their email is your problem.** Ask your attorney, accountant and family office how their own email is protected. Hardware keys and no text recovery is the right answer. An advisor who cannot answer is an advisor whose mailbox is your back door.
- **Watch the deeds.** Sign up for the county recorder's notification service on every property, or a title monitoring service where the county has none. Forged deeds are filed quietly and found late.
- **Freeze your credit** at all three bureaus. It costs nothing and closes the door on loans in your name.
- **Keep the list of who can sign short**, and review it once a year with your attorney. Old authorizations for people who have moved on are the ones nobody is watching.

None of this needs a hardware key. It needs a habit, and it needs the people who work for you to know the habit is not optional.

## For the day you can't

Everyone with a plan like this reaches a day when someone else has to run it. Illness, an accident, a bad year, or simply age. The plan is not finished until that day is covered, and it is far easier to arrange while you are the one arranging it.

The goal is a handoff with no secrets shared in advance, so nobody can quietly take over early, and no scramble at the end, so nobody is locked out. Both halves matter. The first protects you. The second protects the person you choose from suspicion, because they came in through the front door with paperwork.

**Paperwork, with your attorney**

- A durable power of attorney and, for larger estates, a trust with a named successor trustee. Institutions honor these. They do not honor a shared password, and logging in as someone else can break their terms and the law.
- Access to the safe deposit box for the successor, through the bank's own process, tied to that paperwork.
- A short letter, sealed with the handoff key: what accounts exist, who the advisors are, where the password manager is. Accounts and names, not passwords. The passwords are in the manager, and the manager's sentence is in the same envelope.

**In the software**

- Turn on emergency access in the password manager. Name the successor. Set the waiting period to a week or two. They request access; you get notified; if you do not object in time, they are in. If you object, nothing happens.
- Add the successor as the trusted contact at every custodian, and as a recipient of statements.

**A middle step, before the day**

Most people do not go from fully in charge to fully out in one day. Plan for the stretch in between. Give the successor a view of the accounts without the power to move money, and do the monthly review together. When the time comes to hand over the rest, it is a small step, not a cliff.

**Two people, not one**

The successor should not be able to take over alone. Pair them with a second person who has to agree: the attorney, a sibling, a trustee. Where money is concerned, the person most likely to misuse access is someone close, and a second signature protects everyone, including the successor from being accused.

## Do this in order

Do it with one person you trust in the room. Two sittings of an afternoon each, plus one meeting with your attorney.

1. **Buy three keys** and one password manager subscription. Put the manager on your phone and computer.
2. **Identity ring first.** Email, phone carrier, Apple or Google: register all three keys, turn text message recovery off, lock the phone number with the carrier.
3. **Move every password into the manager.** Then destroy the spreadsheet, the notebook, the browser lists, and the other managers. Seal the manager's sentence in the envelope.
4. **Money ring.** Each custodian: keys on, texts off, verbal password, callbacks, lockdown, trusted contact. Get the confirmation in writing. Move anything a custodian will not protect.
5. **The people.** Tell the attorney, accountant and assistant the callback rule. Freeze credit. Sign up for deed alerts.
6. **The handoff.** Attorney meeting: power of attorney, trust, safe deposit box access. Emergency access on in the manager. Envelope and handoff key into the box.

Done when no account holding money can be reset by a text, there is one place passwords live, and one named person could take over next month through the front door without asking you for anything.
